## Abstract

The Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR), the Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR), the Probability of
Backtest Overfitting (PBO) and purged cross-validation are the standard statistical
defenses against backtest overfitting in quantitative finance. This report documents an
open-source implementation of these methods, `algobacktesting`, and the evidence that it
is correct.

1. **Replication.** We reproduce every worked numerical example in the source papers
   that can be computed from printed inputs, at the precision printed.
2. **Proofs.** We prove six properties the implementation relies on. Three of them
   identify input regions where the published formulas silently return wrong answers:
   - an "effective number of trials" between 1 and 2 drives the DSR benchmark to negative
     values, so selection is *rewarded*;
   - excess kurtosis mistakenly supplied for raw kurtosis is detectable through Pearson's
     inequality;
   - the PBO estimator is biased upward for an odd number of strategies (by a factor of
     (N + 1)/N, i.e. 2/3 instead of 1/2 for N = 3).
3. **Errata.** We document five discrepancies in the source papers, all minor, including
   a miscounted number of cross-validation combinations and an approximation-error claim
   we could not reproduce.
4. **Independent checks.** Exact numerical integration, seeded Monte Carlo calibration, a
   rebuilt version of the PBO paper's seasonal experiment, and a brute-force reference
   implementation for purged cross-validation.

Every number in this report is regenerated by `docs/paper/reproduce_numbers.py`.

## 1. Introduction

A researcher who backtests many variations of a strategy and keeps the best one faces a
selection problem. Among enough random strategies, one will show an impressive Sharpe
ratio by luck alone. Bailey and López de Prado address this from two directions:

- **Correct the statistic.** The PSR [1] measures the probability that a strategy's true
  Sharpe ratio exceeds a benchmark, accounting for sample length, skewness and kurtosis.
  The DSR [2] raises that benchmark to the Sharpe ratio expected from the best of N
  skill-less trials.
- **Evaluate the selection process.** The PBO [3] estimates how often the in-sample
  winner underperforms the median strategy out of sample, using combinatorially symmetric
  cross-validation (CSCV). Purged k-fold and combinatorial purged cross-validation
  (CPCV) [4] prevent labels that span time from leaking test information into training.

These methods are widely cited but easy to implement subtly wrong. Units must be
per-period, kurtosis must be raw rather than excess, and purging must use label end times.
This report makes four contributions:

1. **A verified implementation** with 100% line and branch test coverage. Tests pass on
   Python 3.11, 3.12 and 3.14, and CI adds 3.13.
2. **Exact replication** of every worked example in [1]–[3] computable from printed
   inputs (Section 3). Figures built on the authors' proprietary data are out of scope.
3. **Six propositions with proofs** (Section 4). These justify the implementation's guard
   conditions and expose an odd-N bias in the PBO estimator.
4. **Errata** for the source papers (Section 6).

## 2. Definitions and notation

Let $\widehat{SR}$ be a per-period Sharpe ratio estimated from $n$ returns with skewness
$\hat\gamma_3$ and **raw** kurtosis $\hat\gamma_4$ (equal to 3 for a Normal distribution).
$Z$ is the standard Normal CDF.

**PSR** [1, Eq. 11]:
$$\widehat{PSR}(SR^*) = Z\!\left[\frac{(\widehat{SR}-SR^*)\sqrt{n-1}}{\sqrt{1-\hat\gamma_3\widehat{SR}+\frac{\hat\gamma_4-1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2}}\right].$$

**Minimum track record length** [1, Eq. 13], at confidence $1-\alpha$ with
$z_\alpha = Z^{-1}(1-\alpha)$:
$$MinTRL = 1+\left[1-\hat\gamma_3\widehat{SR}+\tfrac{\hat\gamma_4-1}{4}\widehat{SR}^2\right]\left(\frac{z_\alpha}{\widehat{SR}-SR^*}\right)^2.$$

**Expected maximum Sharpe ratio** of $N$ skill-less trials whose Sharpe ratios have
variance $V$ [2, Eq. 2; App. A.1, Eq. 6], where $\gamma\approx0.5772$ is the
Euler–Mascheroni constant:
$$\widehat{SR}_0=\sqrt{V}\left((1-\gamma)Z^{-1}\!\left[1-\tfrac1N\right]+\gamma Z^{-1}\!\left[1-\tfrac{1}{Ne}\right]\right).$$

**DSR** [2, Eq. 2]: $\widehat{DSR}=\widehat{PSR}(\widehat{SR}_0)$.

**PBO via CSCV** [3, Alg. 2.3]:
1. Partition a $T\times N$ matrix of strategy returns into $S$ (even) equal blocks of
   consecutive rows.
2. For each of the $\binom{S}{S/2}$ ways to choose half the blocks as the training set:
   - find the in-sample (IS) best strategy $n^*$;
   - compute its out-of-sample (OOS) rank $\bar r_{n^*}\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ (rank $N$ =
     best), its relative rank $\bar\omega=\bar r_{n^*}/(N+1)$, and the logit
     $\lambda=\ln\frac{\bar\omega}{1-\bar\omega}$.
3. The estimator [3, §3.1] is $\hat\phi=\Pr[\lambda\le 0]$ over combinations.

**Purging and embargo** [4, Ch. 7]: each observation $i$ has a label interval
$[s_i,e_i]$. Training observations whose label interval overlaps a test block are
removed, and an embargo removes the next $h$ observations *after* each test block.
**CPCV** [4, Ch. 12] uses every choice of $k$ of $N$ groups as the test set.

## 4. Propositions

**Proposition 1 (MinTRL inverts PSR).** For $\widehat{SR}>SR^*$ and confidence
$c=1-\alpha\ge\tfrac12$, setting $n=MinTRL$ gives $\widehat{PSR}(SR^*)=c$ exactly. For
$c<\tfrac12$, Eq. (13) returns the track record length for confidence $1-c$ instead.

*Proof.* Write $\sigma^2$ for the bracket in Eq. (13). Then
$n-1=\sigma^2z_\alpha^2/(\widehat{SR}-SR^*)^2$, so
$(\widehat{SR}-SR^*)\sqrt{n-1}/\sigma=|z_\alpha|$, and the PSR equals $Z(|z_\alpha|)$.
This equals $c$ exactly when $z_\alpha=Z^{-1}(c)\ge0$, i.e. $c\ge\tfrac12$. Otherwise it
equals $Z(-z_\alpha)=1-c$. ∎

*Consequence:* the implementation rejects $c<\tfrac12$. A hypothesis property test
confirms the identity to $10^{-9}$ across random inputs.

**Proposition 2 (PSR denominator under Pearson's inequality).** Every distribution
satisfies $\gamma_4\ge1+\gamma_3^2$. Under that inequality the PSR radicand obeys
$$1-\gamma_3 s+\tfrac{\gamma_4-1}{4}s^2\;\ge\;\left(1-\tfrac{\gamma_3 s}{2}\right)^2\;\ge\;0,$$
with equality if and only if $\gamma_4=1+\gamma_3^2$ and $s=2/\gamma_3$.

*Proof.* Substituting $\gamma_4-1\ge\gamma_3^2$ gives
$1-\gamma_3s+\tfrac{\gamma_4-1}{4}s^2\ge1-\gamma_3s+\tfrac{\gamma_3^2s^2}{4}=(1-\tfrac{\gamma_3s}{2})^2$. ∎

*Consequence:* valid inputs can never make the PSR undefined, except on the degenerate
two-point boundary, which the implementation reports. Conversely, a pair violating
$\gamma_4\ge1+\gamma_3^2$ is impossible. Its most common cause is passing **excess**
kurtosis: a Normal's excess kurtosis 0 < 1. Before this check, such input returned a
plausible but wrong PSR, e.g. 0.9437 instead of 0.9430 for $s=0.1$, $n=252$.

Note that the DSR example [2] lies exactly on the boundary: $\gamma_3=-3$, $\gamma_4=10$.

**Proposition 3 (domain of the expected-maximum closed form).** Let
$f(N)=(1-\gamma)Z^{-1}(1-\tfrac1N)+\gamma Z^{-1}(1-\tfrac1{Ne})$ for $N>1$. Then:
- $f$ is strictly increasing;
- $f(N)\to-\infty$ as $N\to1^+$;
- $f$ has a unique root $N_0\in(1,2)$.

Numerically $N_0=1.2836$. For example $f(1.01)=-0.7847$, $f(1.1)=-0.3176$,
$f(1.5)=+0.2159$ and $f(2)=+0.5198$.

*Proof.* Both terms are increasing in $N$ because $Z^{-1}$ is increasing. As
$N\to1^+$, $1-\tfrac1N\to0^+$, so $Z^{-1}(1-\tfrac1N)\to-\infty$, while the second term
tends to the finite $\gamma Z^{-1}(1-e^{-1})$. Continuity, monotonicity and the sign
change between $N=1.01$ and $N=2$ give the unique root. ∎

*Consequence:* for an effective number of trials in $(1,N_0)$, the DSR benchmark is
*negative*. A selection procedure is then rewarded rather than penalized. The
implementation accepts $N=1$ (no selection, $\widehat{SR}_0=0$) or $N\ge2$, and rejects
$1<N<2$.

At $N=2$ the closed form *under*estimates the exact $E[\max]=1/\sqrt\pi=0.56419$ (0.51976).
For $N\ge10$ it overestimates (Section 5.1).

**Proposition 4 (PBO under pure noise; odd-N bias).** Suppose the $N$ strategy columns
are exchangeable and the rows are i.i.d., so no strategy has skill. Then for every CSCV
combination, the OOS rank of the IS winner is uniform on $\{1,\dots,N\}$. Hence
$$E[\hat\phi]=\frac{\lfloor (N+1)/2\rfloor}{N}=\begin{cases}\tfrac12 & N\text{ even}\\[2pt] \tfrac{N+1}{2N} & N\text{ odd.}\end{cases}$$
Under the strict condition of [3, Def. 2.2] ($\bar r<N/2$), the expectation is instead
$\tfrac12-\tfrac1N$ for even $N$ and $\tfrac{N-1}{2N}$ for odd $N$.

*Proof.*
1. The training and testing halves use disjoint rows, so they are independent. $n^*$ is
   a function of the training half only.
2. Conditional on $n^*$, the OOS performances are exchangeable (with probability one,
   untied), so $\bar r_{n^*}$ is uniform on $\{1,\dots,N\}$.
3. $\lambda\le0$ is equivalent to $\bar\omega\le\tfrac12$, i.e. $\bar r_{n^*}\le\tfrac{N+1}2$.
   That event has probability $\lfloor\tfrac{N+1}{2}\rfloor/N$.
4. $\hat\phi$ is an average over combinations, so linearity of expectation gives the
   result. The strict case is identical, with the event $\bar r\le\lceil N/2\rceil-1$. ∎

*Consequence:* with the paper's estimator, pure noise gives $\hat\phi$ of 2/3 for
$N=3$ and 0.6 for $N=5$, not 1/2. The bias is negligible for large $N$, and the
expectation is exactly ½ for even $N$. A single dataset's $\hat\phi$ is not
concentrated, because combinations share data. Over 40 seeded datasets with $N=50$, the
mean was 0.4982 with s.d. 0.1715 (range 0.200–0.886).

**Proposition 5 (purging guarantee).** Let a contiguous test block $B$ have span
$[a,b]$, with $a=s_{\min B}$ and $b=\max_{j\in B}e_j$. Observations are sorted by start
time. If every training observation $i$ satisfies $e_i<a$ or $s_i>b$, then no training
label interval intersects any test label interval. Applying this to each maximal
contiguous test block separately preserves the guarantee for CPCV, without discarding
data between non-adjacent blocks.

*Proof.* For $j\in B$, sorted starts give $s_j\ge a$, and $e_j\le b$ by definition of
$b$, so $[s_j,e_j]\subseteq[a,b]$. If $e_i<a$, then $[s_i,e_i]$ lies entirely before
$[a,b]$. If $s_i>b$, it lies entirely after. In both cases it is disjoint from every
$[s_j,e_j]$. For several blocks, the condition holds for each block, and the test set
is their union. ∎

*Remark:* purging around the span from the first test block to the last would be valid
but wasteful. It discards every observation between non-adjacent test groups.

**Proposition 6 (counting).**
- CSCV evaluates $\binom{S}{S/2}=\prod_{i=0}^{S/2-1}\frac{S-i}{S/2-i}$ combinations [3,
  Eq. 2.3].
- CPCV with $N$ groups and $k$ test groups yields
  $\varphi=\binom{N-1}{k-1}=\tfrac{k}{N}\binom{N}{k}$ backtest paths.
- Assigning path $p$ the $p$-th split (in lexicographic order) that tests each group uses
  every (split, test-group) pair exactly once.

*Proof.* A fixed group is in the test set of exactly $\binom{N-1}{k-1}$ of the
$\binom Nk$ splits, because the other $k-1$ test groups are chosen from $N-1$. So each
group can fill exactly $\binom{N-1}{k-1}$ paths, one per occurrence. Summing occurrences
over groups gives $k\binom Nk=N\varphi$. ∎

## 5. Independent checks

### 5.1 The expected-maximum approximation

The exact $E[\max]$ of $N$ i.i.d. standard Normals is
$\int x\,N\phi(x)\Phi(x)^{N-1}dx$. We computed it on a 240,001-point grid. For $N=100$ we validated it against 20,000
seeded Monte Carlo draws (agreement within 5 standard errors).

| N | Exact | Closed form | Gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 0.56419 | 0.51976 | −0.04443 |
| 10 | 1.53875 | 1.57460 | +0.03585 |
| 46 | 2.21640 | 2.24417 | +0.02778 |
| 100 | 2.50759 | 2.53060 | +0.02301 |
| 500 | 3.03670 | 3.05253 | +0.01583 |
| 1000 | 3.24144 | 3.25512 | +0.01369 |

For $N\ge10$ the closed form overestimates by less than 0.05 and the gap shrinks with $N$,
consistent with [2, App. A.2]. That makes DSR mildly conservative. The magnitude at
$N=1000$ differs from the paper's statement (erratum E4).

### 5.2 PSR calibration under the null

If the true Sharpe ratio equals the benchmark, $\Pr[\widehat{PSR}>0.95]$ should be 0.05.
We ran 4,000 seeded simulations per row, with tolerance 4 binomial standard errors
(±0.0138).

| Returns | n | Moments used | Pr[PSR > 0.95] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | 250 | estimated | 0.0495 |
| BLdP mixture (skew −2.45, kurt 10.16) | 1000 | estimated | 0.0473 |
| BLdP mixture | 1000 | **ignored** (assume Normal) | **0.1505** |

Correcting for skewness and kurtosis is necessary. Ignoring them triples the
false-positive rate on crash-prone returns.

### 5.3 PBO: a rebuilt seasonal experiment

Paper [3, §6] backtests 8,800 monthly trading rules on a random walk, finding a PBO of
55%. With a planted monthly effect it finds 13%. Its random draws are not published, so
we rebuilt the experiment on a smaller grid:
- 48 months of 21 days (1,008 days)
- entry day 1–21, holding period 1–20, long or short, no stop-loss: 840 rules
- $S=16$, i.e. 12,870 combinations
- with an effect, the first five days of each month are shifted by +0.25 s.d.

| Seed | PBO, random walk | PBO, planted effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.258 | 0.085 |
| 2 | 0.640 | 0.165 |
| 3 | 0.430 | 0.003 |

The planted effect lowers PBO on every seed, matching the paper's qualitative
conclusion. The performance-degradation slope was negative throughout (−0.53 to −0.95).
A single strategy with real drift among 49 noise strategies gave $\hat\phi=0$.

### 5.4 Purged cross-validation against a brute-force reference

There is no worked numerical example for purging in the source material available to
this project. So the implementation was compared with an independently written
brute-force reference (explicit loops over all pairs). The comparison covered 500 random
datasets (300 k-fold, 200 CPCV) with irregular and repeated timestamps, label lengths
0–12, $k\in[2,6]$ and embargoes of 0–20%.
- **Training sets:** they matched exactly in every split.
- **Overlap:** no training label overlapped any test label (Proposition 5).
- **CPCV counts:** $\binom{6}{2}=15$ splits and 5 paths, and $\binom{10}{4}=210$ splits
  and 84 paths, as Proposition 6 predicts.

**Comparison with AFML Snippet 7.3.** We encoded the snippet's selection rules, taken
from a secondary reproduction of the snippet:
- keep a pre-test observation if its label end ≤ the test start;
- keep post-test observations from the first one whose start ≥ the latest test label end,
  after skipping $\lfloor n\cdot pct\rfloor$ observations.

On 200 randomized datasets with continuous, tie-free timestamps, these rules give
training sets identical to ours. The rules differ only when labels touch exactly.

That boundary matters. With **zero-length labels** ($e_i=s_i$, e.g. a barrier hit on the
entry bar of daily data), the rules admit **test observations into the training set**:
- the first test observation satisfies $e\le t_0$;
- the post-test search can land on the last test observation.

In our constructed example (10 observations, 2 folds), the snippet's training set
contains test rows 5 and 9. Closed intervals (Proposition 5) exclude this.

**Does purging change the result? A no-skill experiment.** Daily returns are i.i.d.
Normal, the label of day t is the sum of the next 20 returns, and the features are five
trailing moving averages (20 to 320 days). A 5-nearest-neighbour regressor is
cross-validated with 30 folds on 3,000 days. No predictability exists, so the expected
out-of-sample correlation is zero. Averaged over 200 seeded datasets
(`examples/leakage_demo.py`):

| Cross-validation scheme | Out-of-sample correlation (honest value: 0) |
| --- | --- |
| Shuffled k-fold | +0.816 (s.e. 0.002) |
| Contiguous k-fold, no purging | +0.050 (s.e. 0.005) |
| Purged k-fold | +0.000 (s.e. 0.005) |
| Purged k-fold with a 5% embargo | -0.001 (s.e. 0.005) |
| Leak removed by purging (paired difference) | +0.050 (s.e. 0.002) |

The per-dataset standard deviation of the score is about 0.07, larger than the leak
itself. A single backtest therefore cannot reveal the leak, although it biases every
backtest upward. The paired difference isolates it: the unpurged score exceeded the
purged score in 199 of the 200 datasets. A 5% embargo left the purged score unchanged,
as expected for serially independent returns.

### 5.5 Demonstration

Two hundred strategies, each with 1% daily volatility over 1,008 days (seed 42):

| World | Best backtest (annual SR) | DSR | PBO |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| No edge | 1.69 | 0.654 | 0.275 |
| One strategy with true annual SR ≈ 1.3 | 1.69 | 0.642 | 0.274 |
| One strategy with true annual SR ≈ 2.4 | 2.22 | 0.906 | 0.082 |

- **No edge:** luck alone produces an annual Sharpe of 1.69, and DSR rejects it.
- **Modest edge:** it is out-searched, and the winner is still a noise strategy.
- **Strong edge:** it wins and PBO falls to 0.08. But DSR stays below 0.95: after 200
  trials, four years of data do not suffice to establish it.

| # | Source | Statement | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E1 | [3] p. 11 | "if S = 16, we will form 12,780 combinations" | $\binom{16}{8}=12{,}870$ (transposed digits) |
| E2 | [3] Alg. 2.3, step 4(c) | IS performance taken from "the nth column of J (the testing set)" | $J$ is the *training* set; the testing set is its complement |

| E4 | [2] App. A.2 | Overestimation "by 1000 trials … as small as 0.006" (variance 1) | Exact integration gives 0.0137 at N = 1000 (Section 5.1). The paper's heat map is Monte Carlo based, so its noise may explain the difference |

None of these affect the methods' validity. E3 and E5 matter for anyone reproducing the
papers' numbers, and E3 matters for small, odd N.

## 7. Implementation notes and limitations

- **Guard conditions:** the implementation rejects NaN or infinite inputs, impossible
  moments, effective trial counts in $(1,2)$, MinTRL confidence below ½, more than
  $10^6$ CSCV combinations, unequal CSCV blocks, and two-dimensional input where a single
  series is expected.
- **Purged CV:** it is built from the standard definitions in [4]. It was compared with
  a *secondary reproduction* of Snippet 7.3 (Section 5.4) but not yet with the book
  itself. The only difference found is the deliberate closed-interval boundary, which
  also prevents a leak that the snippet's rules allow with zero-length labels.
- **Assumptions:** PSR and DSR assume i.i.d. returns and are asymptotic (about 30+
  observations). Skewness and kurtosis estimated from short samples are noisy.
- **Effective N:** DSR depends on the *effective* number of independent trials, which
  researchers can understate.
- **PBO variance:** a single dataset's PBO has substantial variance (Section 4, Prop. 4).
